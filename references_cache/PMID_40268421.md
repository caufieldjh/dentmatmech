# Accuracy and mechanical properties of denture clasps fabricated utilizing pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs.
**Authors:** Kato Y, Tasaka A, Okano H, Uzawa S, Yamashita S
**Journal:** J Prosthodont Res (2025)
**DOI:** [10.2186/jpr.JPR_D_24_00186](https://doi.org/10.2186/jpr.JPR_D_24_00186)

## Content

PURPOSE: This study evaluated the use of pre-sintered cobalt-chromium (Co-Cr) alloy discs in the fabrication of clasps for removable partial dentures, comparing their accuracy and mechanical properties, as determined via tensile testing, with those of conventional fabrication methods.

METHODS: Accuracy of the clasps was assessed by obtaining three-dimensional (3D) data (design data) of the Akers clasp. The clasps were fabricated under three conditions: (1) pre-sintered, fabricated from pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs; (2) fully sintered, fabricated from fully sintered Co-Cr alloy discs; and (3) cast, fabricated via casting. For each method, 3D data were collected (fabrication data). The design and fabrication data were subsequently superimposed, and the accuracy of the clasps was investigated based on their inner surfaces. The mechanical properties were assessed via tensile testing of dumbbell-shaped Co-Cr alloy specimens fabricated under the same conditions used for accuracy testing (pre-sintered, fully sintered, and cast).

RESULTS: Cast clasps typically exhibited the highest trueness values and fully sintered clasps exhibited the highest precision values. The pre-sintered clasps exhibited high trueness in every part of the rest area; however, the accuracy at the clasp tip was low. In terms of mechanical properties, the pre-sintered clasps exhibited the highest elongation, meeting the standards for Type 4 metals as specified by ISO 22674:2022.

CONCLUSIONS: The present study demonstrates that pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs are suitable for fabrication of clasps with good fit and mechanical properties, provided that the accuracy of the clasp tip is improved.

1. Introduction
In dental prostheses, removable partial dentures are typically 
used to treat patients with various tooth defects, ranging from loss 
of a single tooth to the presence of only one remaining tooth. Treat -
ments involving removable partial dentures are less invasive and 
highly cost-effective than fixed partial dentures or implant-based 
treatments[1–4]. Owing to the recent increase in the price of precious 
metals, cobalt-chromium (Co-Cr) alloys, which are composed of non-
precious metals, have gained traction as retainers for removable par-
tial dentures. The high biocompatibility, good mechanical properties, 
and resistance to abrasion and corrosion of Co-Cr alloys make them 
suitable materials for prosthetic applications[5,6]. Conventionally, Co-
Cr alloy prostheses are fabricated via casting employing the lost-wax 
method. However, this presents several challenges, including less 
precision than those of precious metals owing to the high melting 
point of Co-Cr alloys[ 7, 8], difficulties in post-casting adjustment, and 
high reliance on the skill level of the dentist or dental technician[9].
Computer numerical control (CNC) milling offers a potential 
solution to these issues[ 10], and fixed prostheses fabricated via this 
technique are now widely adopted in clinical practice. Compared 
with casting, CNC milling simplifies the fabrication process and 
reduces inter-operator errors. Among the available methods, fabri -
cation from fully sintered Co-Cr alloy milling discs not only takes ad -
vantage of the milling method described earlier but also ensures the 
fabrication of uniform prostheses without casting defects. However, 
because fully sintered Co-Cr alloy milling discs are harder than discs 
made from other materials, they result in severe abrasion of milling 
burs and require extended processing times[ 11].

In recent years, pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs have been de -
veloped to be soft as wax before final sintering until they undergo 
milling. Consequently, they offer the advantage of minimizing abra -
sion of milling burs and reducing the processing time compared 
with those of fully sintered Co-Cr alloy discs[12]. Several studies have 
evaluated the fit and precision of fixed prostheses fabricated from 
pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs, and the internal and marginal fit of 
crowns fabricated via milling of these discs was similar to that of zir -
conia crowns[13,14]. Implant superstructures fabricated via milling of 
pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs exhibited a similar marginal fit to that 
of implant superstructures fabricated via milling of fully sintered Co-
Cr alloy discs, and a superior fit than that of implant superstructures 
fabricated via casting of Co-Cr alloy ingots[ 15].
For removable partial dentures, one study compared the ac -
curacy of Co-Cr alloy clasps fabricated via various digital techniques. 
Clasps fabricated via milling of fully sintered Co-Cr alloy discs exhib -
ited lower accuracy than those fabricated via casting or selective la -
ser melting[16]. However, the accuracy and mechanical properties of 
clasps fabricated via milling of pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs remain 
unclear.
The objective of this study was to evaluate the use of pre-
sintered Co-Cr alloy discs for clasps in removable partial dentures by 
comparing their accuracy and mechanical properties, as determined 
via tensile testing, with those of clasps fabricated via milling of 
fully sintered Co-Cr alloy discs and clasps fabricated via conventional 
casting. The study assessed two null hypotheses: (1) differences in 
fabrication methods do not change the accuracy of the Co-Cr alloy 
clasps, and (2) differences in fabrication methods do not change the 
mechanical properties of the Co-Cr alloy.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Test specimen fabrication for accuracy evaluation
2.1.1 Acquiring design data from a working model
In this study, a simulated Akers clasp was fabricated for a lower 
left first premolar to treat a Kennedy Class II defect. The Akers clasp 
was selected for this study owing to its ability to simultaneously 
perform the functions of support, bracing, and retention, and is the 
most used clasp in dental practice. After performing blocking and 
relief on a working model (MIS3004-L-PL-28; NISSIN, Kyoto, Japan), 
duplicate impressions were made, followed by the preparation of 10 
refractory casts (Rema Exakt; Dentaurum GmbH & Co. KG, Ispringen, 
Germany). Three-dimensional (3D) data for these refractory casts 
were acquired employing a 3D scanner (EDGE; Denken High Dental 
Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan). Subsequently, Akers clasps were waxed onto 
each refractory cast, and 3D data for both the refractory casts and 
wax patterns of the clasps were acquired utilizing a 3D scanner. 
Using computer-aided design (CAD) software (Free Form; Artec3D, 
Senningerberg, Luxembourg), the 3D data of the refractory casts 
were removed from the acquired data, leaving only the 3D data for 
the wax patterns, which were subsequently used as the design data 
(N=10).
2.1.2. Clasp specimen fabrication
The clasps were fabricated under three different conditions: (1) 
pre-sintered, fabricated from pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs; (2) fully 
sintered, fabricated from fully sintered Co-Cr alloy discs; and (3) cast, 
fabricated via casting. Figure 1  shows a flowchart illustrating the 
clasp fabrication process for each condition, where 10 clasps were

The pre-sintered clasps were fabricated via milling of pre-sin -
tered Co-Cr alloy discs (CopraSintec K; Whitepeaks Dental Solutions 
GmbH, Hamburg, Germany) based on the design data. Computer-
aided manufacturing (CAM) software (inLab CAM SW 19.1; Dentsply 
Sirona, Charlotte, NC, USA) and a 5-axis CNC milling machine (inLab 
MC X5; Dentsply Sirona) were used for fabrication. The design was 
placed on a disc such that the supporting arms were directed toward 
the exterior surface, and eight supporting arms were added ( Fig. 
1). The discs were milled to 8.5% larger than the design data to ac -
count for contraction during the final sintering. After milling, the 
pre-sintered clasps were separated from the discs, and final sintering 
was performed under the following conditions, based on the speci -
fications of the manufacturer: in an argon gas atmosphere at a flow 
rate of 1.1 L/min, with a heating speed of 15 °C per minute, reach -
ing a final temperature of approximately 1280 °C. The pre-sintered 
clasps were maintained at this temperature for 60 minutes, followed 
by unregulated cooling to 800 °C and subsequent cooling with 
compressed air within a closed furnace. After sintering, a carbide bur 
(Bur Carbide HP , SS White Dental, Lakewood, CA, USA) was used to 
remove the connecting portions and refine the shape. Subsequently, 
the clasps were sandblasted (multiblaster CR-III; AWS Co., Ltd., Tokyo, 
Japan) utilizing aluminum oxide powder (050WA, Akiyama Sangyo 
Co. Ltd., Osaka, Japan) at a pressure of 0.5 MPa from a 15 cm distance 
to remove oxides produced during final sintering.
Fully sintered clasps were fabricated via milling of Co-Cr alloy 
discs (Colado CAD Co-Cr 4; Ivoclar Vivadent, AG, Schaan, Liechten -
stein) based on the design data. CAM software (PrograMill CAM V4 
2021; Ivoclar Vivadent, AG) and a 5-axis CNC milling machine (Pro -
graMill PM7; Ivoclar Vivadent, AG) were used for fabrication. After 
milling, the connecting portion was removed, and the shape was 
refined employing a carborundum point (Shofu Carborundum Point 
HP; SHOFU Inc., Kyoto, Japan).
All refractory casts with wax patterns were invested in Rema 
Exakt (Dentaurum GmbH & Co. KG) in accordance with the instruc -
tions of the manufacturer, and cast clasps were fabricated via casting 
utilizing Co-Cr alloy ingots (Remanium GM 800 plus; Dentaurum 
GmbH & Co. KG). A high-frequency centrifugal casting machine 
(DENKO Auto Sensor; DKK Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was utilized for 
casting. The investment material was removed from the cast clasp via 
sandblasting (Sandblaster Forte; ODIC Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) with 
aluminum oxide powder (AWS; ODIC Co., Ltd.) at a pressure of 0.75 
MPa from a 15 cm distance. Subsequently, the sprue and burrs were 
removed utilizing a carborundum point (Shofu Carborundum Point 
HP; SHOFU INC.). After coarse polishing, the clasps were finished via 
electropolishing (Labowada Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) using an electro-
lytic polishing liquid (Electro PS; Dentaurum GmbH & Co. KG). For the 
electrolytic polishing, the continuous electrical current was set to 
4–6 A at 12 V for 5–6 min. Table 1 lists the elemental composition of 
the Co-Cr alloys used for each experimental condition.
2.1.3. Acquisition of clasp data
The clasp surfaces fabricated under each of these conditions 
were coated with alumina powder (pure scan powder; Quest Dental, 
Irvine, CA, USA) and 3D data were acquired using a 3D scanner (ATOS 
core200; GOM, Braunschweig, Germany) for use as the fabrication 
data.
2.1.4. Evaluation of clasp accuracy
Using a 3D data inspection software (GOM Inspect; GOM), the 
fabrication data were superimposed on the design data, and the 
differences in shape were measured. The data superimposition was 
performed using the best-fit algorithm. The values obtained for the 
differences in shape were used to evaluate the trueness (mm) and 
precision (mm) as two indices of accuracy. In this study, trueness is 
defined as the error between the fabrication and design data, and 
precision is defined as the error between the fabrication data for the 
same experimental conditions. The accuracy was evaluated at eight 
points: the rest, proximal plate, shoulders, centers, and clasp tips 
of both the buccal and lingual arms. Five locations were arbitrarily 
designated at each of these points, as shown in Figure 2 , and the 
mean value for these locations was used as the representative value.

2.2.2. Observation of internal defects
Internal defects in the dumbbell-shaped test specimens fabri -
cated under different conditions were observed via micro-computed 
tomography (micro-CT: XT H225 ST; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Micro-CT 
scanning parameters were set at a tube voltage of 220 kV, energy of 
110 μA, power of 24.2 W, and an exposure time of 1000 ms. CT data 
analysis software (VG STUDIO; Volume Graphics GmbH, Heidelberg, 
Germany) was used to measure the total number and total volume of 
internal defects in three dimensions.
2.2.3. Tensile testing
A universal testing machine (Autograph AG-20kNIS; Shimadzu, 
Kyoto, Japan) was utilized to perform the tensile testing of the dumb-
bell-shaped test specimens fabricated under varying conditions at 
a cross-head speed of 1.5 mm/min. A 20-kN load cell (SFL-20kNAG; 
Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used for load measurements at a fre -
quency of 50 Hz. The deformation was measured utilizing a video 
non-contact extensometer (TRviewX 500D; Shimadzu). The nominal 
strain was calculated from the measured strain and initial gauge 
length of the dumbbell-shaped specimen. Stress–strain curves were 
obtained from the measured stresses and nominal strains. These 
curves were subsequently used to calculate the evaluation param -
eters of the mechanical properties: elastic modulus, ultimate tensile 
strength, 0.2% proof stress, and elongation.
2.3. Determination of sample size
To determine the sample size, a power analysis was performed 
using G*Power software (ver. 3.1.9.6; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düs-
seldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) (effect size = 0.6, α = 0.05, power = 0.8, 
number of groups = 3, minimum total sample size = 30). Therefore, 
for each of the three conditions, the sample size was set at 10.
2.4. Statistical analysis
The accuracy and tensile test results were compared across dif -
ferent conditions via the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Bonferroni’s 
adjustment. Internal defects between the pre-sintered and cast 
specimens were compared via the Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical 
analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 21 (IBM, Ar -
monk, NY, USA), with a significance level set at P < 0.05.
3. Results
3.1. Accuracy
3.1.1. Trueness
Figure 4 presents the trueness color map derived via superim -
position. At rest, the most crucial component of the removable par -
tial dentures, represented by the green area, was broader for the pre-
sintered clasps than for the other two types, indicating a high level 
of trueness. Table 2  shows the median, minimum, and maximum 
values as well as the interquartile range (IQR) for trueness at each 
measurement point. The median values for the pre-sintered clasps 
were negative at all measurement points, whereas the median val -
ues of the fully sintered clasps were positive across all measurement 
points. When comparing the median values at each measurement 
point, cast clasps typically exhibited the highest level of trueness, 
followed by the fully sintered and pre-sintered clasps. Notably, the 
pre-sintered clasps demonstrated lower trueness at the tips of both 
the buccal and lingual arms. Significant differences in trueness were 
observed between pre-sintered and fully sintered clasps, as well as 
between pre-sintered and cast clasps at several measurement points.
3.1.2. Precision
Table 3 summarizes the median, minimum, and maximum val -
ues as well as the IQR for precision at each measurement point. The 
results indicated that fully sintered clasps consistently demonstrated 
the highest level of precision, followed by the pre-sintered and cast 
clasps. Notably, the pre-sintered clasps exhibited a lower precision at 
the tip, similar to their trueness results. Significant differences in preci-
sion were observed between pre-sintered and cast clasps, as well as 
between fully sintered and cast clasps, at most measurement points. 
Furthermore, significant differences were observed among all three 
conditions at the proximal plate and shoulder of the buccal arm.

difference in the total volume of the internal defects between the 
two methods.

exhibited high trueness at all measurement points, but exhibited 
lower precision. This indicates that, although cast clasps fit the abut -

trueness at all measurement points, indicating that fabrication data 
errors occurred in a direction that brought the clasps closer to the 
abutment tooth. These errors likely stem from limitations in the end-
mill size, which prevented full milling of the fully sintered clasps in 
accordance with the design data. The fully sintered clasps exhibited 
high precision at all measurement points relative to the other two 
types, demonstrating high reproducibility of their fabrication. In 
comparison, the pre-sintered clasps exhibited negative trueness at 
all measurement points, indicating that fabrication data errors oc -
curred in the direction of the clasp, away from the abutment tooth. 
This error is likely attributed to the contraction of the pre-sintered 
clasps during the final sintering. The trueness of the arm clasp tip 
was particularly low. In addition to trueness, the clasp tip exhibited 
low precision. This indicates that the contraction of the pre-sintered 
clasp during final sintering likely influences the accuracy of the clasp 
tip. However, the color map of overall trueness indicated that the 
pre-sintered clasps exhibited higher trueness than that of the other 
two types, which is critical for the functionality of removable partial 
dentures. A poorly fitting rest adversely affects the durability of re -
movable partial dentures[18]. To reduce the stress on the periodontal 
tissue of the abutment tooth, the rest must transmit occlusal force 
along the longitudinal axis of the abutment tooth[19,20]. A poorly fit-
ting rest fails to transmit the occlusal force appropriately, resulting in 
excessive lateral forces on the abutment tooth, thereby contributing 
to abutment tooth loss[21].
Compared with the fully sintered clasps, the pre-sintered clasps 
exhibited higher trueness at the boundary between the rest and 
proximal plate. This boundary, which features the sharpest angle in 
the design data, is difficult to mill via conventional machining for fully 
sintered clasps[16,22]. However, because the pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy 
discs used in this study were milled 8.5% larger than the design data 
to account for the contraction during final sintering, the ball-end mill 
can more easily reach this angle compared with the fully sintered 
clasps. Therefore, pre-sintered discs are considered more suitable 
than fully sintered discs for milling intricate details such as sharp 
corners. When using the pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs examined 
in the present study to fabricate fixed prostheses exhibiting large 
curvatures of ≥ 5 units, the manufacturer recommends adding a sup-
port structure on the CAD data to prevent unforeseen contraction 
during final sintering. Although clasps are smaller and more flexible 
than fixed prostheses, connecting the clasp tip of the buccal and lin -
gual arms with support can potentially aid in controlling contraction 
during final sintering.
Observations of internal defects via micro-CT indicated that 
the total number of internal defects was the highest in pre-sintered 
specimens; however, the total volume was the highest in cast speci -
mens, and no internal defects were observed in fully sintered speci -
mens. As fully sintered Co-Cr alloy discs are produced by pressing 
under industrial conditions, internal defects are rarely observed[ 23]. 
The large internal defects seen in the cast specimens are likely attrib-
uted to casting defects, particularly those present at the boundary 
between the holding and parallel sections. This boundary, where the 
specimen diameter changes, may have created conditions condu -
cive to blowhole formation, likely resulting from differences in the 
amount of solidification contraction[ 24]. In addition, owing to the 
substantial casting defects observed in the cast specimens, the cast 
specimens in this study exhibited lower elongation than those of the 
pre-sintered and fully sintered specimens[ 25]. In the pre-sintered 
specimens, small defects were scattered throughout. Similar micro -
structural defects were observed in zirconia discs utilized for CNC 
milling, in which final sintering was performed after milling in the 
pre-sintered state[26]. Because pre-sintered zirconia discs contain an 
organic binder to hold the powder together, defects can arise when 
this organic component is removed during sintering[ 27]. Therefore, 
internal defects in the pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy specimens may have 
originated via a similar process.
In tensile testing of the mechanical properties, the cast speci -
mens exhibited the highest elastic modulus and 0.2% proof stress, 
whereas the pre-sintered specimens exhibited the lowest. A higher 
elastic modulus indicates greater rigidity of the clasp, reducing the 
likelihood of deformation. However, excessive rigidity in clasp arms 
result in an excessive retentive force, thereby increasing the load on 
the abutment tooth during insertion or removal of dentures[ 28]. To 
prevent this issue, highly rigid Co-Cr alloy clasps must be designed 
with clasp arms narrower than those made of gold alloys[ 19,20]. 
The lower elastic modulus observed in the pre-sintered specimens 
than those of the other two types indicates that the clasp arms must 
either be thicker or the undercut at the clasp tip must be increased 
to achieve the desired retentive force. Furthermore, the pre-sintered 
specimens exhibited the lowest 0.2% proof stress, indicating higher 
susceptibility to plastic deformation compared with the other two 
types. Therefore, clasps made from pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs re -
quire careful design to prevent plastic deformation. To ensure func -
tional integrity, the rest, which plays a crucial role in supporting the 
denture, must be designed with a greater thickness compared with 
the other two types. The ultimate tensile strength was the highest for 
the fully sintered specimens, indicating that this condition resulted 
in an improved mechanical strength compared with the other two 
conditions. Although the pre-sintered specimens exhibited the low -
est ultimate tensile strength, no significant difference was observed 
between the pre-sintered and cast specimens. In addition, the pre-
sintered specimens demonstrated the greatest elongation, whereas 
the cast specimens exhibited the lowest elongation. The mechanical 
properties and grain size of Co-Cr alloys are influenced by the cooling 
rate after casting and subsequent heat treatment[29,30]. Faster cool-
ing rates after casting result in finer grain sizes, thereby enhancing 
mechanical properties such as ultimate tensile strength but reducing 
ductility. In contrast, heat treatment increases grain size, improving 
ductility while reducing ultimate tensile strength and hardness. In 
this study, the cast specimens underwent slow cooling after casting, 
which likely resulted in larger grain sizes. However, the substantial 
casting defects observed internally likely served as initiation points 
for fractures, resulting in reduced elongation. Additionally, the pre-
sintered specimens in this study were subjected to slow cooling in 
a furnace after final sintering. This process likely produced effects 
similar to those of heat treatment, potentially resulting in larger 
grain sizes. These specimens exhibited the smallest elastic modulus, 
ultimate tensile strength, and 0.2% proof strength among the three 
conditions, indicating the higher elongation. A high elongation rate 
indicates good ductility[ 31], and the combined effect of elongation 
and ultimate tensile strength is a measure of toughness[ 19]. There -
fore, clasps fabricated by milling pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs likely 
exhibits higher toughness and result in clasp arm fracture compared 
with clasps fabricated by casting Co-Cr alloy ingots. This is particu -
larly relevant in clinical practice because clasp fractures can lead to 
premature failure of dentures and increased patient dissatisfaction.
A limitation of this study is that design data were obtained from 
refractory casts for experimental purposes rather than from a work -
ing model. Typically, the wax patterns of clasps on refractory casts 
are assumed to undergo a slight contraction after casting, resulting

in a better fit to the working model. Although the percentage of con-
traction is expected to be minimal and unlikely to significantly affect 
the results of this study, the digital data from clasps accurately fitted 
to the working model must be used as reference data for evaluating 
dimensional accuracy. Additionally, only the accuracy of the Akers 
clasp was evaluated. These results may differ for pre-sintered clasps 
with longer arms, such as bar-type clasps. Further investigations into 
the accuracy of other clasp shapes are necessary. Removable partial 
denture frameworks are larger than simple clasps and contain more 
components that may influence the direction of contraction during 
sintering. Furthermore, repeated insertion/removal tests of pre-
sintered clasps must be conducted in the future to examine changes 
in retention over time. Clinically, such studies are essential for deter -
mining the long-term prognosis of removable partial dentures fabri -
cated using pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs. This study did not directly 
analyze the effects of cooling rates on the grain size, crystallographic 
structure, and mechanical properties of the Co-Cr alloys. Future 
studies must focus on clarifying these relationships using methods 
such as microscopy and electron backscatter diffraction.
5. Conclusions
The present study demonstrated that clasps fabricated from 
pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs exhibited higher trueness across the 
entire rest, as indicated by the color map; however, they exhibited 
lower accuracy at the clasp tip compared with clasps fabricated via 
casting or using fully sintered Co-Cr alloy discs. In terms of their me -
chanical properties, the pre-sintered Co-Cr alloy discs met the stan -
dards for Type 4 metals prescribed by ISO 22674:2022 and exhibited 
high toughness. Therefore, improving the accuracy at the clasp tip 
can potentially enable fabrication of clasps that offer both excellent 
fit and enhanced resistance to clasp-arm fractures.
