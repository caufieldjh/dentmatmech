# Dental human enamel wear caused by ceramic antagonists: A systematic review and network meta-analysis.
**Authors:** Flores-Ferreyra BI, Argueta-Figueroa L, Torres-Rosas R, Carrasco-Gutiérrez RG, Casillas-Santana MA, de Los Angeles Moyaho-Bernal M
**Journal:** J Prosthodont Res (2025)
**DOI:** [10.2186/jpr.JPR_D_23_00263](https://doi.org/10.2186/jpr.JPR_D_23_00263)

## Content

PURPOSE: Fixed restorations and dental enamel have different structures that produce different wear on opposing teeth, resulting in clinical problems. Therefore, it is necessary to determine the type of restoration that causes less wear on naturally opposing teeth to make recommendations. The objective of this study was to systematically analyze the evidence from observational studies and clinical trials on enamel wear in different ceramic restorations.

STUDY SELECTION: The designs of the included studies were randomized clinical trials (RTCs), non-randomized clinical trials (non-RTCs), and observational studies (OS). The studies must answer the research question, be available in full text, be written in English or Spanish, and have had at least six months of follow-up. Protocol number: CRD42023397759.

RESULTS: After screening 499 records, 20 RTCs were subjected to data extraction, 10 were excluded, 10 were included in the systematic review, and only 5 were included in the network meta-analysis. The risk of bias assessment reported moderate to high risk of bias, quality, and certainty of evidence was evaluated and rated as moderate. Network meta-analysis showed higher enamel wear was observed in natural dental enamel against metal-ceramic antagonists.

CONCLUSIONS: Enamel wear occurs in all teeth, even when the antagonist is a natural tooth. The wear is larger on surfaces with the ceramic crown antagonists studied (metal-ceramic, glazed zirconia, and polished zirconia). It is necessary to conduct additional clinical trials with larger follow-up periods and sample sizes.

1. Introduction
Ceramics have been widely used as restorative materials because 
of their desirable characteristics, high aesthetics, biocompatibility, 
chemical stability, mechanical properties, and durability[1]. However, 
there are some concerns regarding the wear compatibility of antago-
nist natural teeth owing to their differences in structure, hardness, 
and surface roughness[2,3]. Dental enamel is highly organized with a 
prism arrangement, whereas dental ceramics are a group of materi -
als with different structures, such as feldspathic, leucite, and lithium 
disilicate[4], which are composed of a glassy matrix (amorphous) and 
some crystalline fillings that are prone to dislodge from the matrix 
and act as a third body, affecting surface integrity[5,6]. Other ceramic 
materials, such as alumina and zirconia, have crystalline structures; 
consequently, their mechanical properties and surface characteris -
tics differ. The contact and friction between two structures of differ -
ent natures lead to a specific interaction, resulting in wear. Dental 
wear is a complex process involving friction, environmental pH, 
chewing frequency, chewing force, dietary habits, and the type of 
dental material used for restoration. Enamel wear can trigger clinical 
problems, such as damage to dental tissues on the occlusal surface

In the literature, many in vitro studies used different laboratory 
assessment methods[9 –11], but it is difficult to replicate the natural 
oral conditions and teeth anatomy in this type of study. It has been 
reported that some factors could influence the wear of enamel 
produced by antagonists, such as the nature of the material, surface 
roughness, and surface finishing. Similarly, in vitro studies have re -
ported that polished surfaces produce less enamel wear even less 
than feldspathic porcelain. It has been postulated that under in vitro 
conditions, polished zirconia shows less wear on antagonist’s teeth 
than the enamel itself, which has raised doubts about whether this 
also occurs in patients[ 12]. However, in a systematic review, it was 
not appropriate to compare the results of different study designs[13]. 
Analysis of the results of observational studies and clinical trials is 
possible because all these studies were conducted in humans. Some 
systematic reviews included all types of study designs and invalid 
comparators, which could have led to incorrect conclusions. To pre -
vent this, measuring dental enamel wear as a comparator in the 
control and intervention groups (as an antagonist) and performing a 
network meta-analysis between the interventions is necessary. This 
analysis could be useful for the clinician’s material selection and in -
forming the patient which material would generate less wear on the 
natural antagonist tooth. Therefore, the objective of this study was 
to systematically analyze the evidence from observational studies 
and clinical trials on enamel wear with different ceramic restorations.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Protocol registry and reporting
This systematic review was registered in the International 
Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database [registration 
number. CRD42023397759, available at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/
prospero/display_record.php?ID=CRD42023397759]. For reporting, the 
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis 
(PRISMA) and Cochrane Handbook were followed[14,15].
2.2. Eligibility criteria
Eligibility criteria were defined according to the PICO (Popula -
tion, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcomes) strategy. The included 
studies were randomized clinical trials (RTCs), non-randomized clini -
cal trials (non-RTCs), and observational studies (OS). The studies must 
answer the research question, be available in full text, be written in 
English or Spanish, and have had at least six months of follow-up. The 
inclusion criteria were adult patients (> 18 years old) who required 
full crown restorations in natural teeth, in the maxillary or mandibu -
lar arch of the molars or premolars and included at least one group 
with full crowns of zirconia, lithium disilicate, or metal ceramic. In ad-
dition, studies must determine enamel wear in the natural tooth as 
an antagonist and in a control group with enamel/enamel occlusion. 
Studies with implant-supported crowns and those with the design of 
clinical case reports, case series, editor letters, short communication, 
in vitro studies, animal studies, comments, narratives, scoping, or 
systematic reviews were excluded.
2.3. Information sources and search strategy
An electronic search was conducted between February and March 
2023. No restrictions on publication time were imposed in the search. 
The search strategy was built using keywords and mesh terms identi-
fied by the PICO strategy ( Table 1) in five databases: Google Scholar, 
PubMed, ClinicalTrials. gov, Scopus, and Web of Science.
2.4. Selection process
The eligibility of the studies was determined by reading the title 
and abstract of each document retrieved during the search. Subse -
quently, the full texts of studies that met the eligibility criteria were

investigator (LAF). Studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria 
were excluded with reasons.
2.5. Data collection process and data items
A standardized worksheet was created to register data of inter -
est from the selected studies, and data extraction was performed 
independently by one reviewer (BIFF) and reviewed by a second 
reviewer (LAF). Information on the year of publication, author, 
design, population, sample size (n), intervention (crowns), compara -
tor (control enamel), main outcome (enamel wear), and secondary 
outcome (restoration wear) was retrieved. When data were missing, 
the corresponding author of the selected study was contacted to 
request the missing data or information.
2.6. Study risk of bias and certainty assessment
Two researchers (BIFF and LAF) performed the risk of bias assess-
ment of the included studies. The instruments used to perform such 
assessments were the ROBINS-I tool[ 16] for non-randomized clinical 
studies and RoB 2.0[ 17] for randomized clinical trials. The quality 
of included studies was assessed using the Confidence in Network 
Meta-Analysis (CINeMA) for multiple interventions compared[ 18,19]. 
Disagreements were resolved by consensus of the research group.
2.7. Effect measures and synthesis methods
The effect measure of the main outcome was the mean differ -
ence. Two reviewers (BIFF and LAF) created a table to summarize 
the quantitative findings for each main outcome from the included 
studies.
The data were grouped according to the material of the full 
crown, which was zirconia, lithium disilicate, and metal-ceramic. The 
follow-up time was considered to perform the quantitative synthesis. 
The main outcome was enamel wear measured in microns; in cases 
where the studies used another measurement unit, they were con -
verted to microns. The reported mean of the main outcome (enamel 
wear) as vertical height loss in µm and SD was used to perform 
the network meta-analysis, and the heterogeneity was calculated 
through Ʈ2. Inconsistency between the direct and indirect estimates 
was estimated. A sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the 
robustness of the synthesized results when there was a different risk 
of bias between studies. A league table was constructed to present 
the direct and mixed (network meta-analysis) estimates. In addition, 
a Bayesian random effects model forest plot and ranking probability 
graph were represented by the surface under the cumulative ranking 
curve (SUCRA). R software (R Development Core Team, 2011. Version 
4.2.0) and CINeMA online software (available at https://cinema.ispm.
unibe.ch/) were used to perform the aforementioned calculations 
and generate graphs of the network meta-analysis.
2.8. Reporting bias assessment
For publication bias assessment, Funnel Plot Asymmetry and 
the Likelihood-ratio Test were planned to be performed if sufficient 
studies were included.
3. Results
3.1. Study selection
A total of 527 studies were initially retrieved after electronic 
searching, and two records were found by hand search; of these, 28 
records were duplicated, 499 were screened by title and abstract, 
and 479 were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion cri -
teria. Twenty records were retrieved in full-text. However, 10 studies 
were excluded with reasons: three had duplicated data[ 20–22], six 
did not have a comparator[23–28], and one did not have an adequate 
comparator according to the inclusion criteria[ 29]. Finally, 10 articles 
were included for qualitative synthesis; of these three studies were 
randomized clinical trials[ 30–32], two were split-mouth randomized 
clinical trials[33,34], three were non-randomized clinical trials[35–37], 
and two studies split mouth non-randomized clinical trials[ 38,39]. 
Only five studies were included in the quantitative synthesis (net -
work meta-analysis). The PRISMA flowchart is shown in (Fig. 1).
3.2. Study characteristics and results of individual studies
The studies included in this systematic review were those with at 
least six months of follow-up[ 38] and a maximum of 36 months[ 30]. 
All included studies had a control group as a comparator, and the 
control group consisted of natural teeth with enamel/enamel oc -
clusion in the contralateral or contralateral antagonist. Two studies 
accepted small restorations in the natural antagonist; the only condi-
tion was occlusal contacts on the enamel[ 30,38].
The study’s sample size ranged from 10 to 43 patients, and the 
number of restorations ranged from 13 to 60 crowns per study. The 
ages of the participants ranged from 18 to 73 years, and both sexes 
were included.
The studies had very similar participant characteristics: good 
general health, absence of active periodontal disease and caries, 
good hygiene, normal saliva flow, no medical, mental, or sleep 
disorders, no evidence of temporomandibular disorders, or para -
functional habits (bruxism, grinding, clenching). Only 2 studies used 
specific inclusion criteria, such as attrition score >2[ 36,38],1 study 
measured muscle pain and muscle activity in a second step selec -
tion by a disposable electromyography device, subjects with severe 
muscle activity were excluded[ 38], 3 studies excluded patients with 
non-developmental enamel defects, fluorosis, calcium metabolic 
disorders or osteoporosis[ 33,34,39] only 1 study specified that in -
cluded teeth were endodontically treated molars and premolars, and 
clenching force capability was determined by the use of a bite force 
gauge[35], and 2 studies considered a root ratio of at least 1:1, bite 
force measured using a bilateral gnathodynamometer[ 30,31].
In this review, it was found that the studied materials were 
monolithic lithium disilicate crowns, core lithium disilicate veneer 
crowns, metal ceramic feldspathic veneer crowns, and the most 
frequently studied were polished and glassed monolithic zirconia 
crowns. A total of 309 crowns were included in the studies selected 
for this systematic review.
In most studies, the wear parameter for measurement was ver -
tical loss (µm). Only 2 studies reported volume wear (mm 3)[35,37]. 
This process was performed by comparing the baseline models with-
those of each recall time. The indirect method was used to collect 
wear information using polyvinylsiloxane impressions and poured

3.3. Risk of bias in studies
Regarding the risk of bias, 60% of the clinical randomized trials 
included were classified as having some concerns[ 32–34], and 40% 
had a high risk due to no data reported[ 30] and SD values[ 31]. For 
non-randomized clinical trials, 80% of the studies exhibited a moder-
ate risk of bias, and 20% had a serious risk. The risk of bias evaluation 
is shown in (Figs. 2A-B).
3.4. Certainty of evidence
As described previously, the quality of the included studies was 
assessed using the CINeMA tool[ 18,19] and the confidence ratings 
was moderate (Table 3).

The risk of bias in the included studies was moderate to high. 
The strength of the trial design was that the measurements were 
performed using digital scanning and software, and the split-mouth 
design, which reduced the risk of bias, allowed the data of these trials 
to be pooled.
The certainty of the evidence was evaluated using the CINeMa 
tool[18,19], which is based on the GRADE assessment; however, for 
the GRADE assessment, items are built to some extent on apply -
ing for direct evidence[ 43]. Rating the confidence in the evidence 
for a network of interventions is more challenging than pairwise 
meta-analysis; as a result, the CINeMa tool was used to estimate the 
intervention effect from a network meta-analysis as a weighted sum 
of all available direct comparisons. The moderate certainty of the 
CINeMA was mainly attributable to the risk of bias. Moreover, other 
factors might have contributed to diminishing the certainty of the 
evidence, such as the small sample size and short follow-up time in 
the reported studies.
Regarding enamel wear, it is expected that high hardness pro -
duces more antagonist wear; however, there are more important 
factors, such as structural stability and surface roughness.
It has been reported that there are no significant differences in 
the enamel wear produced by metal-ceramic crowns[ 30]; however, 
in the present network meta-analysis, the results showed that metal-
ceramic crowns produced larger enamel wear in the natural an -
tagonist[33,39]. A possible explanation could be the structure of the 
veneer ceramic, which is a glassy matrix, where crystalline particles 
are embedded, which is prone to microfracture with the occlusal 
load; consequently, the detachment of some glassy structures could 
act as a third body, and crystal exposure increases the roughness and 
together promotes surface wear[44].
The second material with the highest enamel wear was glazed 
zirconia. The glaze material is applied in a very thin layer to fill the 
irregular surface and provide a glassy, smooth, brilliant surface; 
however, it is prone to dislodge quickly, exposing the rough surface 
of zirconia[12,45].
Polished zirconia exhibited the least wear of the antagonist 
enamel. It is important to mention that even though it shows less 
enamel antagonist wear than metal ceramics and glazed zirconia, it 
still shows significant clinical differences when compared with natu -
ral enamel antagonist groups[25].
In general, for lithium disilicate crowns, there is no agreement in 
the results; one study reported that the mean occlusal wear volume 
of ceramic molar crowns was significantly lower than that of the op -
posing natural teeth, but there was no difference in contralateral an -
tagonist wear[30,35 ]. In contrast, statistically significant differences 
have been reported when comparing the natural enamel antagonist 
wear of lithium disilicate crows against that of controls[32].
Other systematic reviews have been published; however, their 
analysis was different from that in the current review. One of these 
reviews that included both in vitro and clinical studies could not 
perform a meta-analysis or reach conclusions[ 46]. In a meta-analysis 
of in vitro studies including metal-ceramic, glazed, and polished zir -
conia full crowns, one conclusion was that polished zirconia causes

A plausible reason for the different results exhibited across the 
studies could be the follow-up period of each included study. It has 
been suggested that running-in wear in the first 12 months after the 
restoration placement is followed by steady-state wear after two 
years. That is, a rate of enamel wear occurs at a greater speed from 
the placement of the restoration until the occlusion reaches dynamic 
equilibrium, which occurs in a period between 1 and 2 years[ 42]. In 
the present review, it was not possible to statistically compare the 
enamel wear produced by the disilicate crowns for the missing re -
port of some data, authors were intended to be contacted, but there 
was no answer[30] due to that there is the need of more clinical trials 
to reach solid conclusions.
One limitation of the present study was that it did not include 
metal antagonist surfaces to compare enamel antagonist wear. Al -
though ceramics appear to be the first-choice restorative material, 
metal–ceramic crowns are highly recommended for specific clinical 
situations.
Therefore, enamel wear must be considered as a complex 
mechanism. It depends on individual-specific physiological mecha -
nisms, dental tissue properties, and the structure of the restorative 
materials. In this review, we found no specific information regarding 
the composition or specific properties of zirconia in any of the stud -
ies. The crystalline structure of zirconia is modified according to its 
generation[5]. It is important to report the specific characteristics 
of restorative materials in clinical studies to better understand wear 
results and mechanisms. In addition, it is necessary to carry out more 
clinical trials to contribute more evidence with respect to the enamel 
wear mechanism, with an adequate sample size and a large follow-
up period, to be able to provide long-term evidence.
5. Conclusions
The following conclusions can be drawn within the present 
review’s limitations. Enamel wear occurs in all teeth, even when the 
antagonist is natural. The wear is larger on surfaces with the ceramic 
crown antagonists studied (metal-ceramic, glazed zirconia, and 
polished zirconia) than on natural enamel. The wear values from 
highest to lowest are metal-ceramic, glazed zirconia, and polished 
zirconia crowns. The clinician must use their judgment to determine 
the appropriate material depending on the patient’s characteristics, 
and it is preferable to use polished zirconia crowns instead of glazed 
ones. It is necessary to conduct more clinical trials with an adequate 
sample size and follow-up period to provide more evidence with 
respect to the enamel wear mechanism.
